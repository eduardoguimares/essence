# Theoretical perspectives on power

## French and Raven's bases of power

- Legitimate power - derived from formal authority (manager's position)
- Coercive power - relies on the use of punishment or negative consequences
- Referent power - linked to personal charisma and admiration
- Reward power - based on the ability to provide rewards or incentives
- Expert power - arises from knowledge, skills or expertise

## Jeffrey Pfeffer's power tactics

- Networking - building influential relationships
- Coalitions - forming alliances to amplify influence
- Control of resources - controlling critical resources
- Information control - access to valuable information

## Steven Lukes' three dimensions of power

How power operates in a society
1. Visible power - overt decision-making authority
	- Visible power is exercised by influenced public statements like company policies, strategies and decisions
2. Hidden power - shaping agendas and controlling information behind the scenes
3. Ideological power - influencing beliefs, norms and values
	- Ideological power can be exerted by shaping culture by setting examples and promoting certain ideals

Similar to the [[Levels of organizational culture]]

## Michel Foucault's discourse and power

- Ubiquitous power - exists everywhere, not just in formal institutions or structures
- Discursive power - operates through language and discourse. It shapes our understanding of reality, constructs identities and influences behavior
- Panopticon[^1] metaphor - surveillance and self-regulation. It regulates behavior when no one is watching
	- Self-censorship - people avoid expressing controversial opinions, fearing backlash
	- Conformity - Individuals adjust their behavior to fit societal expectations
		- Dressing professional for work
		- Avoiding taboo topics
	- Internalized norms - policing ourselves because we've internalised societal norms
		- Avoiding [[Jaywalking]]
		- Maintaining personal hygiene
		- Speaking politely
	- ---
	- Nowadays, this form of power is applied through social norms
	- This form of power is practiced nowadays in China with the high-tech surveillance

# Historical perspectives

## Early control mechanisms

- Hierarchical Authority - In ancient societies control was often centralized in the hands of rulers, monarchs like the Pharaohs, kings, and emperors, or religious leaders. Hierarchies were rigid, and decisions flowed from the top down
- Centralized Decision-Making - Key choices, such as resource allocation, laws and military strategies, were made by a select few often distant from the people they governed. Monarchies, feudal systems, and religious institutions exemplified centralized control
	- Bureaucratic structures enforced compliance

## Shift to Adaptive Approaches

- Human Relations Movement - emphasized employee well-being (Elton Mayo’s Hawthorne Studies in the 1920s)
	- It marked a shift from rigid control to considering social dynamics
- [[Quality circles]] - employee involvement in decision-making
- Matrix Organizations - Companies moved away from strict hierarchies
	- It allowed dual reporting lines, fostering collaboration

## Contemporary Trends

- Decentralization - Decision-making authority is distributed. Teams have autonomy to adapt to local needs
- Agile Methodologies - Agile emphasizes flexibility, iterative processes, and self-organizing teams
- Digital Transformation - Technology enables real-time data, remote work, and decentralized control
- Holacracy - Some organizations experiment with self-management and distributed leadership

# Resistance

## Negative resistance

Actions taken by individuals or groups within an organization to impede or obstruct the normal functioning or progress of work processes
- Sabotage - employees intentionally slowing down production or damaging equipment to protest
- Work-to-rule - strictly adhering to rules which often results in a slowdown or hindrance to operations
- Whistleblowing - Exposing unethical practices despite potential backlash

## Productive resistance

Actions taken by individuals or groups within an organization that aim to bring about positive change, improve working conditions, or challenge existing norms in constructive ways
- Voice - employees expressing concerns constructively, suggesting improvements
- Innovation - challenging existing norms by proposing creative solutions
- Unionization - collective bargaining for better working condition

# Traditional control modes

- Bureaucratic control - relies on strict rules, procedures, and formal hierarchies to manage employee behavior
	- Policies and Procedures - organizations create guidelines for employee conduct, such as dress codes or safety protocols
	- Job Descriptions - detailed job descriptions outline tasks and responsibilities for each position
	- Performance Evaluations - regular assessments measure employee performance against predefined standards
- Output control - focuses on measurable results and outcomes
	- Sales targets
	- Customer satisfaction metrics
	- Production output - monitoring the number of units produced per week
- Behavioral Control - emphasizes monitoring and influencing employee actions
	- Attendance Policies - tracking employee attendance and addressing absenteeism
	- Quality Inspections - Regular checks to ensure adherence to quality standards
	- Surveillance Systems - monitoring employee behavior through security cameras

# Informal control

- Social norms - unwritten rules that influence behavior
- Peer pressure - colleagues shaping each other's actions
- Cultural values - shared beliefs impacting [[Organizational culture]]

[^1]: Jeremy Bentham's concept of a prison design where a central watchtower allows for constant surveillance of inmates
